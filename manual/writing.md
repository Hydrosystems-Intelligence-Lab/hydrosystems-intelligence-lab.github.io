## Manuscripts

A strong manuscript should make the research question, contribution, evidence, and limitations easy to find.

Before requesting a full review, check:

- The abstract states the problem, method, main result, and implication.
- Figures are readable without relying on the text.
- Methods include enough detail for reproduction.
- Claims are supported by results.
- Limitations and assumptions are explicit.

## Proposals

A strong proposal makes the gap, hypothesis, method, feasibility, and impact clear. Keep the connection between the scientific question and the proposed work visible throughout the document.

For fellowship and grant drafts, start earlier than feels necessary. Proposal quality improves with several rounds of narrowing.

## Internal Review

When asking for feedback, state what kind of feedback you need:

- Scientific framing
- Methods and assumptions
- Figures and tables
- Writing clarity
- Journal fit
- Final proofreading

## Peer Review

Reviews should be rigorous, constructive, and specific. Focus on the validity of claims, clarity of evidence, reproducibility, and whether conclusions are supported by the analysis.

## Presentations

For talks, lead with the research problem and why it matters. Keep methods tied to decisions the audience needs to understand.

## Review Etiquette

When reviewing someone else's draft, separate major scientific issues from line edits. Start with the most important comments: framing, methods, evidence, figures, and conclusions. Detailed wording edits are useful only after the structure works.
